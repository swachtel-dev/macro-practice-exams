Item 42 pts
KLO-9 U13
A city imposes a maximum legal price on gasoline that is well below the current market-clearing price. Holding everything else fixed, what should we predict?

Why:

A maximum price set below the market-clearing price creates excess demand at the cap. At the lower legal price, buyers want more gasoline than they did before. Sellers offer less than they did before. The gap shows up as a shortage: queues at the pump, rationing, "out of gas" signs, sales to favored customers, side-channel transactions at unofficial prices. The price ceiling did not change anyone's underlying preferences or production capacity. It changed who got the gasoline at what posted price.

Item 62 pts
KLO-28 U13
The Federal Reserve buys $50 billion in Treasury securities from primary dealers through an open-market operation. Holding everything else fixed, what is the immediate effect on bank reserves and the money supply?

Why:

When the Fed buys $50 billion in Treasuries from primary dealers, it pays for them by crediting the dealers' bank accounts with new reserves. Bank reserves in the system rise by $50 billion. With more reserves to lend, banks expand new loans, which become new deposits at other banks, which become more loans (the money multiplier). The money supply expands. This is the classic expansionary open-market operation, used by the Fed when it wants to push the federal funds rate down and stimulate borrowing.

Marcus is offered three ways to spend his Saturday afternoon: a paid catering shift worth $150, a friend's birthday brunch he values at 80, or a freelance writing job worth 120. He takes the catering shift. What is the opportunity cost of the catering shift?

Why:

Item 166 pts
KLO-15 U11
The textbook notes that US presidents must be careful in public statements about the economy: pessimistic talk can lower confidence, reduce household and firm spending, lower velocity growth, shift AD inward, and contribute to causing the recession the president warned about. Using the dynamic AS/AD model, walk through the mechanism step by step. Then contrast this Keynesian (demand-driven) view with the classical (supply-determined) view, in which output is determined by real factors regardless of demand.
When you have a draft, reveal the sample answer to compare.

Sample answer:

A pessimistic public statement lowers consumer and business confidence. Households expect harder times and cut spending; firms expect lower future revenue and postpone investment. Both effects lower the velocity of money (%ΔV falls). By the identity %ΔM + %ΔV = π + Yr, spending growth falls. AD shifts inward. The new short-run equilibrium has lower real growth and lower inflation. Unemployment rises. The recession the president warned about has happened in part because of the warning. This is the demand-driven (Keynesian) story: nominal spending decisions in the present can drive the real economy's growth rate in the short run because of sticky wages and money illusion. The classical (Say's law) view holds that supply creates its own demand and that real output is determined by the economy's real productive capacity (LRAS), not by spending decisions. In the classical world, a confidence drop that lowered AD would only lower the price level (or inflation), not real output: the economy would still grow at its potential rate. The dynamic AS/AD model splits the difference. In the long run, LRAS dominates: the economy grows at its potential growth rate regardless of demand. In the short run, sticky wages and money illusion mean that AD shifts can move real growth, so the Keynesian story is right for the time horizon over which contracts are sticky. Both views are present in the same diagram, on different time horizons.

Key points an answer must hit:

  • Mechanism: confidence drops, %ΔV falls, AD shifts inward, real growth + inflation both fall
  • Identifies as Keynesian (demand-driven, sticky-wages-relevant)
  • Contrasts with classical Say's law: LRAS determines output, AD shifts only affect inflation
  • Synthesis: short-run Keynesian, long-run classical, both in the same diagram

Grading notes:

3 pts for the mechanism walk-through (1 pt confidence to spending, 1 pt to AD-left, 1 pt to equilibrium effect). 1 pt for noting the asymmetric effect when starting in flat SRAS. 2 pts for correctly identifying as Keynesian and contrasting with Say's law.

Item 196 pts
KLO-3 U3
An airline has spent 2 million on advertising and gate-leasing fees to launch a new route between two mid-sized cities. After three months, ticket revenue on each flight averages 18,000 and the cost of flying each flight (fuel, crew, cleaning) averages 15,000. An executive argues the route should be cancelled because the 2 million launch cost has not been recovered. In 4 to 6 sentences, explain whether the marginal decision rule supports the executive's reasoning. Name what is sunk, what is marginal, and which numbers belong in the decision.
When you have a draft, reveal the sample answer to compare.

Sample answer:

The $2 million in launch and gate-leasing fees is sunk. It is already paid and cannot be recovered whether the route continues or stops. The marginal rule says: compare marginal revenue and marginal cost of the next flight, not the historical cost of starting the route. The marginal revenue of one more flight is 18,000. The marginal cost is 15,000. 18,000 minus 15,000 is $3,000 of marginal profit per flight, so each additional flight adds to the airline's total profit. The executive is committing the sunk cost fallacy: treating the 2 million as if it were a cost of the next flight. Cancelling the route does not recover any of the 2 million; it forfeits the $3,000 per flight of marginal profit. The airline should keep flying the route while marginal revenue exceeds marginal cost.

Key points an answer must hit:

  • 1 pt for identifying the $2 million as sunk; 2 pts for naming the marginal comparison; 1 pt for the correct numerical comparison; 1 pt for the correct conclusion; 1 pt for naming the executive's error.

Item 2115 pts
KLO-15 U12
Fiscal policy (Congress and the Treasury) and monetary policy (the Federal Reserve) can pull in the same direction or work against each other. Write a structured essay (350-500 words) covering: (1) Define one historical example where fiscal and monetary policy worked together (the 2008-09 response is acceptable). (2) Define one historical example where fiscal expansion was partially neutralized by monetary tightening ("monetary offset"). (3) Explain why coordination matters in the AS/AD framework. (4) Identify one structural reason genuine coordination is difficult in the US system.
When you have a draft, reveal the sample answer to compare.

Sample answer:

The 2008-09 response is a clear case of fiscal and monetary policy pulling together. Congress passed ARRA: roughly $830 billion of tax cuts, transfers, and direct spending, all aimed at pushing AD right. At the same time, the Federal Reserve cut the federal funds rate to near zero and began large-scale asset purchases (quantitative easing), also aimed at pushing AD right through the interest-rate and asset-price channels. Both tools, same direction. The combined effect was a larger rightward AD shift than either could have produced alone, and the response was matched to the diagnosis (an AD-collapse recession in the flat region of SRAS). The early-1980s case is the canonical example of monetary offset. Reagan-era fiscal policy ran large deficits through tax cuts and defense spending, which on its own would have pushed AD right. Paul Volcker's Fed was simultaneously running the tightest monetary policy in modern U.S. history, raising the federal funds rate above 19 percent at one point to crush inflation expectations that had become un-anchored in the 1970s. Monetary contraction reversed a large part of the fiscal AD push. The net effect on AD was much smaller than the fiscal expansion alone would suggest. The deficits drove real interest rates very high, crowded out interest-sensitive investment, and contributed to the early-1980s recession that finally broke inflation. In the AS/AD framework, coordination matters because both fiscal and monetary policy act primarily on the AD curve. When they push together, the AD shift is the sum (roughly) of the two pushes. When they push apart, much of one cancels the other, and the policy mix mostly affects the composition of demand (more government, less interest-sensitive private investment) rather than its total. Genuine coordination is difficult in the U.S. system by design. The Federal Reserve is independent: the president appoints governors to 14-year terms and the Federal Reserve Act insulates the Board from direct political instruction. The reason for that independence is historical and important. A Fed that takes orders from Congress is a Fed that monetizes debt when convenient, which produces the kind of inflation-expectation un-anchoring that the 1970s demonstrated. The cost of independence is that fiscal and monetary policy are pursued by two institutions answering to different mandates and different time horizons. Coordination happens through communication and shared analytical frameworks, not through a single decision-maker, and during disagreements the result is the kind of offset the early 1980s showed.

Key points an answer must hit:

  • 2008-09 = coordinated expansionary fiscal plus monetary, both shifted AD right
  • early-1980s = fiscal expansion (Reagan) plus monetary contraction (Volcker), monetary offset
  • in AS/AD: both tools move AD; coordination amplifies, conflict cancels
  • Fed independence is by design (insulated from political pressure)
  • coordination is informal at best in the US

Grading notes:

Out of 15 pts: 3 pts for the coordinated example with mechanism; 3 pts for the monetary-offset example with mechanism; 3 pts for the AS/AD framing of why coordination matters; 3 pts for the structural reason (Fed independence); 3 pts for clarity and synthesis.

Demand-side evidence: $5 trillion in cumulative fiscal support during 2020-21 directly raised household nominal spending power; pent-up savings unwound rapidly as the economy reopened; headline unemployment fell below 4 percent (a tight labor market is incompatible with a demand shortfall). The clean AS/AD diagnosis is a rightward AD shift into a constrained SRAS: demand surged into a supply curve that could not fully respond, producing price increases much larger than the corresponding quantity increases. The honest lesson is that macro policy is not 'loose is always good' or 'tight is always good' — the right move depends on the diagnosis.

Sample answer:

Four-step move: in an emergency such as a major transit shutdown, what changed is rider circumstances. Buses are not running, so riders who would have taken the bus now want a rideshare ride instead. The price of rideshare itself did not change first; rider circumstances did. Who is affected: buyers. Direction: at every fare, more riders want a ride than before. With supply (drivers logged on) roughly fixed in the very short run, the new equilibrium has a higher price. A rider who could walk twenty minutes, share a ride with a friend, or wait an hour will think twice when she sees a 3x multiplier. The same surge price tells drivers that a ride right now is unusually valuable, and is worth coming in from across town or interrupting an evening to provide. The number of rides provided would almost certainly be lower than under surge pricing, because drivers would have less reason to log on during a difficult period. The available rides would be rationed by waiting time and luck rather than by willingness to pay. Some riders who valued the ride very highly (a parent picking up a sick child, a worker who absolutely had to get home) would not get one, while others who happened to open the app at the right moment and were content to wait would. Many riders would be worse off, even though those who got a ride paid less. The visible cost (the surge price) is replaced by an invisible one (long waits, unmet rides, lottery-style distribution).

KLO-37 U3
Two hypothetical countries, Alpha and Beta, started in 1960 with roughly identical PPFs (same population, same resources, same technology). By 2020, Alpha's PPF is roughly four times the size of Beta's: Alpha can produce four times as much of any combination of goods. In 250 to 400 words, do three things: (1) name the three standard categories of cause that can shift a PPF outward over time and give a concrete example of each; (2) explain why a recession in Beta is not a candidate explanation for the long-run gap (be specific about what a recession does to the PPF picture); (3) name one ceteris paribus assumption the PPF model holds constant when used this way, and explain why naming it matters for interpreting the comparison.
When you have a draft, reveal the sample answer to compare.

Sample answer:

A PPF shows the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce given its resources, technology, and institutions. When Alpha's frontier ends up four times the size of Beta's after six decades of identical starting conditions, the explanation has to be something that shifts the frontier outward, not something that moves an economy around inside it. The three standard categories of cause that shift a PPF outward over time are: more resources, better technology, and better institutions. More resources includes physical capital accumulation (machines, buildings, infrastructure) and human capital (schooling, training, health). Concrete example: Korea's investment rate ran above 30 percent of GDP for decades and its school-enrollment rates approached universal secondary by the 1980s. Better technology means new ways of combining inputs to produce more output (semiconductors, container shipping, mRNA vaccines). Concrete example: the diffusion of computer technology raised manufacturing productivity across the OECD in the 1990s. Better institutions means the rules of the game: property rights, enforceable contracts, rule of law, low corruption, predictable politics. Concrete example: post-1990 Chile preserved private property and judicial independence while neighboring Venezuela did not, and the growth paths diverged accordingly. A recession is not a candidate explanation because a recession is a temporary movement inside the PPF, not a smaller PPF. When unemployment rises and factories run below capacity, an economy is producing below what its frontier allows; the frontier itself has not moved. Even a deep recession lasts a handful of years, while the Alpha-Beta gap accumulated over sixty. A multi-decade four-fold productive capacity gap requires the frontier itself to have moved, not just the operating point inside it. One ceteris paribus assumption: the PPF holds population size constant across the comparison. Naming this matters because the meaning of 'four times the size' changes depending on what is fixed. If Alpha's population is four times Beta's, the per-capita gap disappears. If populations are similar, the per-capita gap is real and large. The PPF picture is silent on which case applies, so the reader has to label the assumption to know whether the comparison is about country-level output or living standards.

Key points an answer must hit:

  • three causes named: resources, technology, institutions
  • concrete example for each cause
  • recession is inside-frontier, not a smaller frontier
  • gap is too large/long-run to be a recession
  • one ceteris paribus assumption named
  • reason naming it matters

Grading notes:

4 pts for the three causes (1 pt each for naming, 1 pt for at least two concrete examples); 5 pts for the recession analysis (3 pts for the inside-vs-shift distinction, 2 pts for explaining why recession does not fit a 60-year gap); 4 pts for the ceteris paribus section (2 pts for naming an assumption, 2 pts for explaining why naming it matters); 2 pts for writing quality and staying within word count.
